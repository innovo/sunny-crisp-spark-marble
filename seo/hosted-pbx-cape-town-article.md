# Hosted PBX in Cape Town: A 2026 Buyer's Guide for Growing Businesses

*By [Author name, role at Innovo Networks] · Updated 31 October 2026* **[VERIFY: add a named author with relevant experience]**

A **hosted PBX** is a business phone system that runs in a provider's data centre instead of in a box in your server room. Your desk phones, softphones and mobile app connect to it over the internet. The provider handles the hardware, the software updates and the connection to the public phone network, and you pay a monthly fee per user.

For most South African businesses with 50 to 300 staff, a hosted PBX now costs less to run than an on-site system and is easier to manage across branches and remote staff. Whether it works *well* comes down to two things buyers often treat as separate purchases: **the internet connection underneath it** and **how well the system is secured**. This guide covers all three together, because in practice that is how they succeed or fail.

## What is a hosted PBX?

PBX stands for private branch exchange: the switch that routes calls between your extensions and out to the phone network. With a *hosted* PBX (also called a cloud PBX), that switch is software running on servers in a data centre. Calls travel as data using SIP (Session Initiation Protocol) over your internet link.

Day to day, staff keep the features they expect: extensions, ring groups, call transfer, voicemail-to-email, auto-attendants ("press 1 for accounts") and call recording. They also gain features that are awkward on old hardware. A single number can ring a desk phone and a mobile app together. Branches in Cape Town, Johannesburg and Durban can sit on one system with free internal calls. Call reports are available from a browser.

## Is a hosted PBX right for a 50–300 person business?

### When it's the right fit

- **You have more than one site, or people who work away from a desk.** Branches, depots, field sales and hybrid staff all join one system without separate hardware at each location.
- **Your current PBX is near the end of its life.** A failing on-site system usually means paying for a hardware replacement, a maintenance contract and someone in-house who understands it.
- **You're still on copper lines.** Openserve, Telkom's wholesale arm, is retiring copper area by area and has said it cannot give fixed dates for specific areas. Moving to hosted voice on fibre now lets you choose the timing instead of reacting to a decommissioning notice.
- **You want predictable costs.** Per-user monthly pricing turns a capital purchase into an operating expense that scales up or down with headcount.

### When it isn't

A hosted PBX is the wrong choice if your site can't get a stable, low-latency internet connection and you have no realistic backup link. That rules out some remote mining and oil-and-gas sites. It can also be the wrong choice if you have recently bought an on-site PBX with years of support left. In that case, consider connecting your existing system to SIP trunks and moving fully to hosted later. And if your organisation already lives in Microsoft Teams, look at **Teams Direct Routing**, which puts proper PSTN calling inside Teams, alongside a standalone hosted PBX. *[VERIFY: link to your Teams Direct Routing page if you offer it.]*

## What does a hosted PBX cost in South Africa?

Published South African pricing for cloud PBX extensions starts at roughly **R50–R70 per user per month excluding VAT** for basic seats. Feature-rich or contact-centre seats cost R180–R300 or more. For example, Euphoria Telecom publicly lists R65 per extension for its entry tier and R295 per agent for its contact centre product, and BizVoIP lists R49 per user. Treat these as market reference points, not like-for-like quotes: what each seat includes varies widely.

### The five cost lines to compare

When you put quotes side by side, compare these five lines, not just the headline seat price:

1. **Seat (licence) fee:** per user, per month. Check which features are in the base tier, especially call recording, reporting and mobile apps.
2. **Call charges:** per-minute rates, or an "unlimited local calling" bundle. Ask what counts as local, whether mobile calls are included, and what fair-use limits apply.
3. **Once-off setup:** configuration, porting fees and training.
4. **Handsets and headsets:** buy, rent, or use softphones only. Contact-centre staff usually need good USB headsets whichever option you choose.
5. **Connectivity:** the business fibre line and backup link the phones depend on. Many quotes leave this out, yet it is the cost that most affects call quality.

### Worked example: 120 users over five years

The figures below are **illustrative assumptions**. Replace them with your own quotes. The point is the method.

| Cost line | Assumption | 5-year total (excl. VAT) |
|---|---|---|
| Seats | 120 users × R150/month × 60 months | R1,080,000 |
| Calls | R6,000/month bundle × 60 months | R360,000 |
| Setup & porting | Once-off | R25,000 |
| Handsets | 80 desk phones × R1,800 (40 staff on softphones) | R144,000 |
| Connectivity uplift | Extra R2,500/month for a backup link × 60 | R150,000 |
| **Total** | | **R1,759,000** |
| **Per user per month** | R1,759,000 ÷ 120 ÷ 60 | **≈ R244** |

To compare this with keeping an on-site PBX, cost the same five years:

- **Hardware:** a replacement system plus a refresh partway through.
- **Maintenance:** the annual maintenance contract.
- **Lines and calls:** line rental and per-minute calls from your current carrier.
- **Staff time:** the internal time spent on adds, moves and changes.
- **Downtime:** the cost of an outage if the single box fails.

The per-user-per-month figure is the number to compare across providers, because it puts pricing models that look nothing alike on the same footing.

## Your internet connection decides your call quality

Most complaints about hosted phone systems ("robotic voice", "calls dropping", "I can't hear them") are network problems, not PBX problems. Voice needs very little bandwidth, but it is sensitive to **latency, jitter and packet loss**, especially when it shares a line with large uploads or video calls.

### How much bandwidth do you need?

A call using the G.711 codec, the uncompressed standard, uses about **87 kbps in each direction** once network overhead is included. Wideband G.722 is about the same. Opus can use far less. You need enough for your **peak concurrent calls**, not your total headcount. In a typical office, 20–30% of staff on a call at the same time is a common planning figure. A contact centre can approach 100%.

| Staff | Planning peak (concurrent calls) | Voice bandwidth needed (each direction, G.711) |
|---|---|---|
| 50 | 15 | ~1.3 Mbps |
| 120 | 36 | ~3.1 Mbps |
| 300 | 90 | ~7.8 Mbps |
| 120-seat contact centre | 120 | ~10.4 Mbps |

The numbers are small, but they need protecting. In practice, that means:

- **Symmetrical business fibre**, so uploads don't choke your outbound audio.
- **Quality of Service (QoS)** on your router and switches, so voice traffic goes first.
- **A separate voice VLAN**, so phones aren't competing with laptops on the same network segment.

### Failover: what happens when the line drops

One of the real advantages of hosted voice is that the PBX keeps running in the data centre even when your office is offline. Plan for it:

- **Failover routing:** if the office can't be reached, inbound calls divert automatically to mobiles, another branch or voicemail.
- **A backup link:** a second fibre provider or LTE/5G router on automatic failover, sized for at least your voice traffic.
- **Mobile apps:** staff who carry the softphone can keep working on mobile data.

### Power in 2026: past load shedding, not past outages

Hosted PBX marketing leaned hard on load shedding, and that argument has aged. Eskom reported more than 400 consecutive days without load shedding by June 2026 and projected none for winter 2026. Even so, local "load reduction" outages still hit some areas, municipal faults happen, and Eskom has warned of a tighter supply outlook from around 2029. A hosted PBX moves the critical component into a data centre with its own backup power. Your office still needs a **UPS on the fibre terminal (ONT), router and PoE switch**, or desk phones go dark with the lights.

## Hosted PBX security: the risk most buyers overlook

A phone system is internet-facing infrastructure, and attackers treat it that way. The three threats that matter most for South African businesses:

- **Toll fraud:** criminals break into a poorly secured PBX account and route thousands of rand of premium-rate or international calls through it, often overnight or over a weekend. You get the bill.
- **Vishing (voice phishing):** attackers spoof caller ID to pose as a director, a bank or "IT support" and talk staff into payments or password resets.
- **Account takeover:** a compromised admin login lets an attacker redirect calls, forward the CFO's line or quietly listen to recordings.

The financial stakes are real. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach study put the average breach in South Africa at **R44.1 million**, and at **R70.2 million in the financial sector**. Not every incident involves the phone system, but voice is now a routine part of social-engineering attacks.

Controls to insist on, from your provider or your own IT team:

- **Multi-factor authentication** on the admin portal and the user apps.
- **International and premium-rate dialling** blocked by default, opened only for users who need it.
- **Daily spend limits and fraud alerts** that suspend unusual call patterns automatically.
- **Strong, unique SIP credentials** per device, plus IP restrictions where practical.
- **Encrypted signalling and media (TLS and SRTP)** where the handsets support them.
- **Removing leavers on the same day** they leave, for both extensions and app logins.
- **A call-back verification rule** for any payment or banking-detail change requested by phone, backed by staff training on vishing.

This is where buying voice and cybersecurity from separate suppliers creates gaps. Someone has to own the firewall rules, the voice VLAN, the endpoint security on softphone laptops and the monitoring, as one design. *[VERIFY: link to your cybersecurity / managed security service page.]*

## Compliance: call recording, POPIA and financial-sector rules

*This section is general guidance, not legal advice. Confirm your obligations with your compliance officer or legal adviser.*

**Call recording (RICA).** The Regulation of Interception of Communications and Provision of Communication-Related Information Act, 2002 allows recording in two main ways. Section 5 covers recording with a party's prior written consent. Section 6 is a business exception: you may record communications made in the course of business if, among other conditions, you make reasonable efforts to inform people in advance that calls may be recorded. In practice, that means:

- an announcement on inbound calls;
- a recording clause in employment contracts and customer terms;
- a written record of how you notify people.

**POPIA.** Recordings, call logs and caller numbers are personal information. You need a lawful basis, a defined retention period, access controls and a hosting location you can justify. Ask your provider where recordings are stored and who can reach them. Since **1 April 2025**, a security compromise involving personal information must be reported to the **Information Regulator through its eServices portal**. POPIA sets no minimum risk level for reporting, so your provider's incident process needs to feed yours.

**Financial services.** The FSCA and Prudential Authority's **Joint Standard 2 of 2024 (Cybersecurity and Cyber Resilience)** came into force on **1 June 2025**. Commentators reported a 12-month window to comply in full. It covers governance, security controls, testing and incident reporting at regulated financial institutions, and your telephony provider and the systems it touches fall inside that scope. Financial services providers also have record-keeping duties for client interactions under FAIS, so check that your retention settings match. *[VERIFY the specific retention period with your compliance adviser before stating it.]*

## How to migrate without losing calls or numbers

You can keep your existing numbers, including 021 geographic numbers and 087/086/080 non-geographic numbers, under ICASA's number portability regulations. Non-geographic porting became fully available on 7 March 2022. Number blocks have to be ported together. Timelines depend on the losing provider: allow anything from about a week to several weeks, and don't book a hard cutover date until the port is confirmed.

A low-risk migration usually follows this sequence:

1. **Audit (week 1).** List every number, extension, ring group, auto-attendant menu, fax line, alarm or lift line, and any integration (CRM, call recording, wallboards). Alarm and lift lines often still need analogue handling.
2. **Network readiness (weeks 1–2).** Confirm fibre capacity, set up QoS and a voice VLAN, install a UPS, and order a backup link.
3. **Build and test (weeks 2–3).** The provider configures the new system on temporary numbers. Test call flows, recording and mobile apps with a pilot group.
4. **Port request (weeks 2–6).** Submit porting paperwork early. It needs an account holder's signature and the losing provider's account details.
5. **Cutover.** Phones are live and tested before the port completes. When the numbers move, calls simply start arriving on the new system. Schedule this for a quiet period, and have the provider on standby for the first business day.
6. **Clean-up.** Cancel old line rentals only after the port is confirmed, so you don't pay twice or lose a number.

## Seven questions to ask any hosted PBX provider

1. **Where are the platform and the call recordings hosted, and can I get that in writing?**
2. **What uptime does the SLA commit to, and what credit do I get when it's missed?** Ask for at least 99.9% for the platform.
3. **What exactly does the seat price include?** Recording, reporting, mobile apps, queues?
4. **What fraud controls are on by default?** Spend caps, international blocking, MFA?
5. **Who is responsible when call quality is poor: you, my ISP, or my IT provider?** One accountable party saves weeks of finger-pointing.
6. **What's the contract term and the exit process?** Can I port my numbers away without penalty?
7. **Can I speak to a reference customer in my industry and of my size?**

## Frequently asked questions

**How much does a hosted PBX cost in South Africa?**
Basic cloud PBX seats are publicly listed from about R50–R70 per user per month excl. VAT. Feature-rich and contact-centre seats run to R180–R300 or more. Once you add calls, handsets and connectivity, total cost for a mid-sized business usually lands well above the headline seat price. Compare quotes on a five-year, per-user basis.

**Can I keep my 021 number if I move to a hosted PBX?**
Yes. Geographic numbers such as 021 can be ported to a new provider, as can 087, 086 and 080 numbers. Number ranges must move as a block.

**What happens to my phones if our internet goes down?**
The hosted PBX keeps running in the data centre. With failover routing set up, inbound calls divert to mobiles, another branch or voicemail. With a backup LTE or second fibre link, the office keeps calling as normal.

**Is call recording legal in South Africa?**
Generally yes, for business purposes, provided you meet RICA's conditions. In practice that means informing callers and staff in advance that calls may be recorded. Recordings are personal information under POPIA, so they need secure storage and a defined retention period.

**Is a hosted PBX secure?**
It can be more secure than an ageing on-site system, because the provider patches the platform. The main risks are weak passwords, open international dialling and social engineering. Insist on MFA, spend limits, fraud alerts and staff vishing awareness.

**Hosted PBX or Microsoft Teams Phone?**
If your staff already work in Teams all day, Teams with Direct Routing puts calling where they already are. If you need advanced call-centre features, receptionist consoles or many desk phones, a hosted PBX is usually the better fit. Some businesses run both, connected together.
